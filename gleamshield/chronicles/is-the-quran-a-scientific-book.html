Is the Quran a Scientific Book?

By Angel Kanu — November 2, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Angel Kanu tests nine popular “scientific miracle” claims, from expanding space and embryology to 360 joints, against the verse itself, the classical tafsir, and what was already known before the 6th century.
  • One test runs through the article: if something was already known in the 6th century, such as altitude sickness, fresh and salt water, or the sun’s movement, it cannot be a prophecy or scientific miracle.
  • The tafsirs quoted for Surah 51:47 and Surah 21:30 speak of the heavens and of rainfall, not of an expanding universe or the Big Bang.
  • Mathematical patterns prove little: similar patterns can be found in the Book of Mormon and in the Bible.
  • Her aim is not to say the Quran is entirely wrong, but that people are often shown only the parts that seem true.

The development of cosmological models in ancient Greece saw early ideas challenging a stationary Earth, with Pythagoreans like Philolaus and Hicetas in the 5th century BCE proposing that the Earth revolved around a central fire. Two centuries later, Aristarchus of Samos advanced this by suggesting a heliocentric model where planets orbited the Sun. However, these heliocentric ideas struggled to gain traction due to the inability to explain the lack of observable stellar parallax.

This led to the dominance of the geocentric model, most notably formalized by Ptolemy in the 2nd century CE. His mathematical system, detailed in the Almagest, used epicycles and deferents to accurately predict celestial motions, including retrograde movement. This geocentric system, which placed a stationary Earth at the center of the universe, persisted for approximately 1400 years.

The shift back towards heliocentrism began in the 16th century with Nicolaus Copernicus, whose work De revolutionibus orbium coelestium re-established the Sun-centered model. Galileo Galilei's support for this model famously led to his trial.

Learn how many joints are in the human body and how they support movement and flexibility.

We understand the challenge of determining exact figures for anatomical structures. Medical definitions vary widely: some experts count every bone intersection, while others focus only on movable connections. Sesamoid bones, like those in the kneecap, further blur the lines between tendons and skeletal components.

Age plays a critical role in these calculations. As children grow, cartilage transforms into solid bone, reducing total counts by nearly 25%. These developmental changes mean joint numbers fluctuate throughout life, making standardized measurements difficult.

The human skeleton’s adaptability adds another layer of complexity. Joints evolve to support movement patterns, heal after injuries, and compensate for wear. This dynamic nature explains why estimates range from 250 to 350 connections—a spectrum reflecting both biological diversity and diagnostic interpretation.

There are a lot of reasons why the it’s taken that the total number of joints in the human body is 360: In antiquity, the human body was frequently viewed as a microcosm of the universe (the idea that man mirrors the heavens). Frequently Asked Questions

Does the Quran predict that the universe is expanding?

The article argues it does not say that. Surah 51:47 speaks of the heavens, and the tafsir quoted explains it as the vastness of space and the heavens being made high. The next verse, 51:48, turns to the earth being spread out. Angel Kanu calls reading it as a prediction of expansion eisegesis.

Does the Quran describe embryology accurately?

The article says no. Galen wrote about embryology in the 2nd century, so fetal development was not unknown in the 6th century. It also asks how a chewed lump of flesh becomes bone, and notes that the tafsir places a man's sperm in his back and a woman's egg in her chest, which is not how the body works.

Did the Quran predict things like mountain sickness, fresh and salt water not mixing, or the sun's orbit?

The article applies one test: was it already known before the 6th century? Altitude sickness is described in the Book of Han and by Xuanzang, Aristotle and Pliny wrote about fresh and salt water, and Greek astronomers had debated the movement of the sun and the Earth centuries earlier. If it was known, she argues, it cannot be a prophecy or a scientific miracle.

Did Muhammad split the moon?

The article gives three reasons to doubt it. No verse says the moon rejoined, and the Quran records people asking for signs in Surah 29:50-51, 6:37 and 21:5. NASA scientist Brad Bailey is quoted as saying there is no scientific evidence of the Moon being split, and an AFP fact check explains that the lunar feature often cited, Rima Ariadaeus, is a geological rille.

Do number patterns in the Quran prove it is from God?

The article says they do not. It shows that the same kind of patterns can be found in the Book of Mormon and in the Bible, so patterns alone cannot prove that a book is from God, since many of them depend on the person finding them.
