Were the Writers of the Gospel Originally Anonymous?

By Angel Kanu — November 9, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Not every scholar says the Gospels are anonymous. Leaving your name out of the text was normal for ancient writers like Plutarch, Philo and Tacitus.
  • No Gospel manuscript names a different author. The only one without a title, P1, has simply lost its heading.
  • Forgers seeking authority would have chosen Peter, Paul or James — not a tax collector and two men who were never apostles.
  • Papias, Irenaeus, Clement, Tertullian, Origen and the Muratorian Fragment all name Matthew, Mark, Luke and John.
  • The objections about Matthew’s Hebrew, Mark’s use of Peter, John 21:24 and Gaius of Rome do not overturn that witness.

One day I approached a Muslim guy and preached to him about the love of God, and one of his objections is what I want to discuss in this blog. Of course, I have heard other Muslims make the same claim, but he went a bit farther in the claim.

So he said that the people who wrote the Gospels weren’t even disciples of Jesus, and I replied and told him that’s not true, because the Gospel according to Matthew was written by Matthew the tax collector, a disciple of Jesus, and also that according to John was written by John the beloved, a disciple of Jesus.

And yes, Mark and Luke were not disciples of Jesus, but they knew the disciples of Jesus.

Then the Muslim guy went further to say that there is no scholar that agrees with the fact that the writers of the Gospels are actually the writers, that every scholar says that the writers are actually anonymous.

And this is the subject of our discussion today. Now in reality his claim is not true, and when he says every scholar, I know he was actually referring to Bart Ehrman, because that is the scholar most Muslims would like to bring up to refute Christianity. And it is interesting that Bart Ehrman himself believes Jesus died historically, but he has doubts that Jesus actually rose again. Also, Bart Ehrman was once a Christian who became an atheist, and the reason he became an atheist was not because he didn’t think Christianity was convincing enough; it was solely because, according to him, he wasn’t satisfied with the response to the problem of evil in the world.

And again, why I also know he was talking of Bart Ehrman was because he asked me if I knew who he was, and when I said yes, he was a bit surprised.

Anyways, I said all that to say that in reality, not every historian believes the writers of the Gospels are actually anonymous. But let’s go through Bart Ehrman’s claim and other scholars’ claims.

The Claim: “Completely Anonymous”

“The first thing to emphasize about Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John is that all four are completely anonymous. The authors never indicate who they are. They never name themselves. They never give any direct, personal identification of any kind whatsoever.”— Bart D. Ehrman, Jesus Before the Gospels, p. 106
“Nowhere in their texts do any of the four gospels name their writers.”— Randel McGraw Helms, Who Wrote the Gospels?, p. 3

Now the problem with this claim is that most scholars are trying to use our own modern-day idea of how a book should be written and expect it to be exactly the same as that of ancient times. Because there are people who didn’t mention their names in their work, for example:

  • Philo omitted his name from his biographical works on Abraham, Joseph, and Moses.
  • Plutarch makes no mention of his name in his Parallel Lives.
  • Porphyry does not state he is the author of any of his surviving works.
  • Philostratus does not mention his name in his biographical work.
  • Nepos does not mention his name in his biographical work.
  • Tacitus leaves his name out of his biography of his father-in-law.

Even the New Testament scholar Michael Licona agrees with this and says:

“It was not unusual for ancient authors to leave their names out of their works. Plutarch was a Greek author who penned more than 50 known biographies during the late first to early second centuries. Plutarch’s name is absent from all of them. It is the tradition that has been passed down through the centuries that gives us information pertaining to who wrote these biographies. No one questions that Plutarch is the author.”— Michael R. Licona, “Fish Tales: Bart Ehrman’s Red Herrings and the Resurrection of Jesus,” in Come Let Us Reason, p. 140

Were the Names Added in the Second Century?

Now apart from this, it is interesting to know that if we say the writers of the Gospels were originally anonymous and 2nd-century writers added the names later on to give the writings more credibility, as some scholars would say:

“There were some books, such as the Gospels, that had been written anonymously, only later to be ascribed to certain authors who probably did not write them (apostles and friends of the apostles).”— Bart D. Ehrman, Jesus, Interrupted, pp. 101–102
“…all four of the canonical gospels were individually anonymous. It was only in the second century CE, when the four gospels were published as a collection that the superscriptions were added to the gospels, attributing authorship to Matthew, Mark, Luke and John respectively.”— Colleen M. Conway and David M. Carr, A Contemporary Introduction to the Bible: Sacred Texts and Imperial Contexts, 2nd ed., p. 233
“We believe that all four Gospels were originally published anonymously, and the names they now bear were given to the four books later in order to link the books to disciples of Jesus or close disciples of disciples of Jesus…”— Dale B. Martin, New Testament History and Literature, p. 20

This is a huge claim worth thinking about. And what do I mean? First of all, there is no manuscript that shows any of the Gospels without names except P1, where the Gospel according to Matthew doesn’t have its name on it, and that’s because its heading is missing.

Apart from this, imagine that these Gospels were being read and accepted by people. So imagine these people were reading something without knowing who the author of what they were reading was.

As some New Testament scholars would say:

“To suggest that no titles whatsoever were added to the Gospels until the late second century AD completely fails to take into account the fact that multiple Gospels were already circulating before Luke ever set pen to papyrus, and that there would be a practical need to identify these books.”— Brant Pitre, The Case for Jesus, p. 20
“When Gospels left their original contexts and were read elsewhere, it is hard to imagine at least some hearers not thinking ‘Says who?’ … Even before the presence of multiple Gospels in one congregation, Christians might well have wanted to know where a [Gospel] came from. Hence it is not necessarily the case that names must only have become a concern at the earliest when two or more Gospels were gathered together.”— Simon Gathercole, “The Alleged Anonymity of the Canonical Gospels,” JTS 69, no. 2, pp. 447–476
“It is perfectly normal for literary works to begin without a reference to their author. The author’s name should already be known to the reader or hearer from the usual devices.”— Simon Swain, “The Hiding Author,” p. 55

So let’s take a look at the Gospels individually and address claims in favour of and against the anonymity of the Gospels.

The Gospel According to Matthew

Why Would Matthew Write in the Third Person?

First of all, let’s consider the Gospel according to Matthew. Most times the claims people have against Matthew are, first, that the author of the Gospel according to Matthew didn’t indicate who he was, and secondly, that if Matthew (the disciple of Jesus) was actually the author, why would he write in the 3rd person? As Michael Kok would say:

“Matthew as a character in the text never speaks in the first person. The reader does not get the impression that Matthew was narrating the events in the Gospel.”— Michael J. Kok, Tax Collector to Gospel Writer, p. 23

The fact that Matthew wrote in the third person is not peculiar to Matthew alone, because even in the Gospels, John the beloved also spoke in the third person.

And Michael Licona noted this:

“…Julius Caesar does not identify himself as the author of his Commentaries on the Civil War, and he writes entirely in the third person, as the author of John’s Gospel may have done.”— Michael Licona, “Are the Gospels ‘Historically Reliable’?”, p. 5

Also,

“The absence of a name within the body of an ancient work is entirely understandable because of all the other ways in which the author might be identified. There were of course numerous ways of indicating an author’s name in or on a roll or codex, outside of the work itself.”— Simon Gathercole, “The Alleged Anonymity of the Canonical Gospels”

Although the name of the author of the Gospel according to Matthew wasn’t written, it was obviously known who wrote it whenever it was read amongst the church. Also, it would be absurd that if second-century Christians wanted to ascribe the Gospels to people for authority, Matthew, a tax collector, would be one of them. Normally, if someone wanted to do that, I would expect it to be the Gospel according to Peter, Paul, James or John, as these were the leading men.

The Marks of a Tax Collector

Also, from the Gospel we can see that there are things that show it was actually written by a tax collector (because he mentioned money the most), for example:

The accounts of Matthew 20:1-13, Matthew 18:24-28 and Matthew 17:24-27 concerning money were documented by Matthew alone.

Another is Matthew 10:9:

“Provide neither gold nor silver nor copper in your money belts,” — Matthew 10:9 (NKJV)

Matthew specifically listed the coins, “gold, or silver, or copper,” unlike the other Synoptics:

“He commanded them to take nothing for the journey except a staff—no bag, no bread, no copper in their money belts—” — Mark 6:8 (NKJV)
“And He said to them, ‘Take nothing for the journey, neither staffs nor bag nor bread nor money; and do not have two tunics apiece.’” — Luke 9:3 (NKJV)

The American theologian and scholar of Greek and the New Testament, Edgar Goodspeed, said:

“Certainly if one demands a signature of Matthew the tax collector, this looks very much like it. … We are struck with several features of the Gospel of Matthew that decidedly favor tax-collector authorship.”— Edgar J. Goodspeed, Matthew: Apostle and Evangelist, pp. 59–60

Early Witnesses to Matthew

Apart from these, there are early attestations that noted the fact that the Gospel according to Matthew was indeed written by him. For example:

“Therefore, Matthew put the sayings (logia) in an ordered arrangement in the Hebrew language, but each person interpreted them as best he could.”— Papias (AD 60–139), quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.16
“Matthew also issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect, while Peter and Paul were preaching at Rome, and laying the foundations of the Church. After their departure, Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, also handed down to us in writing what had been preached by Peter. Luke also, the companion of Paul, recorded in a book the Gospel preached by him. Afterwards, John, the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon his breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus.”— Irenaeus (AD 115–180), Against Heresies 3.1.1
“The first [Gospel] written was that according to Matthew, who was once a tax collector but afterwards an apostle of Jesus Christ, who published it for those who from Judaism came to believe, composed as it was in the Hebrew language. Secondly, that according to Mark, who composed it in accordance with the instructions of Peter… And thirdly, that according to Luke, for those who from the Gentiles came to believe. After them all, that according to John.”— Origen (AD 185–253), quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 6.25
“I lay it down to begin with that the documents of the gospel have the apostles for their authors… In short, from among the apostles, John and Matthew implant in us the faith, while from among the apostolic men Luke and Mark reaffirm it.”— Tertullian (AD 155–220), quoted in Quasten, Patrology II/4:246–340
“It is said that he (Pantaenus) went to the Indians, and the tradition is that he found there that among some of those there who had known Christ from the Gospel according to Matthew which had preceded his coming; for Bartholomew, one of the apostles, had preached to them and had left them the writing of Matthew in Hebrew letters.”— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 5.10.3

Was Matthew Written in Hebrew or Greek?

Now although there is external evidence showing that Matthew the tax collector was indeed the author, here comes another objection most scholars present: it is said that Matthew was written in Hebrew, but the Matthew we have is written in Greek. As some scholars clearly state:

“It is worth noting that the two pieces of solid information that Papias gives us about Matthew are not true of ‘our’ Matthew. Our Matthew is not just a collection of Jesus’ sayings, and the Gospel was certainly written in Greek, not Hebrew. Has Papias simply gotten his information wrong? Or is he simply talking about some other book written by Matthew — for example, a collection of Jesus’ sayings — that we no longer possess?”— Bart D. Ehrman, Jesus, Interrupted, p. 109
“But the Gospel of Matthew is written in Greek. … Nor is Matthew just ‘Logia’ (sayings). Whatever Papias means, it is not the Gospel of Matthew.”— Randel McGraw Helms, Who Wrote the Gospels?, p. 41
“Papias then says that Matthew composed his Gospel in Hebrew and then translated various times into Greek, but that also can’t be true, because Matthew copies extensively from Mark’s Greek, frequently verbatim, and thus can’t possibly have been composed in Hebrew. It’s clear that Papias had no idea what he was talking about, and had no reliable sources of information about the first century of Christianity.”— Richard C. Carrier, On the Historicity of Jesus, pp. 325–326

So how do we reconcile the fact that early writings let us know that the Gospel according to Matthew was written in Hebrew, but the Matthew we have is in Greek?

The best reconciliation is that Matthew originally wrote in Hebrew, and then after Mark wrote his own, Matthew published a new Greek Gospel where he utilised Mark, because it came from Peter’s eyewitness account, and he combined it with his Hebrew work.

As Craig Blomberg would support:

“Indeed, although it goes beyond anything we can demonstrate, it has been plausibly suggested that this first draft of Matthew was what scholars call Q. Then either Matthew himself or some other Greek-speaking Christian would have later incorporated into this first draft elements of Mark along with more distinctive traditions (‘M’) to create the Greek version of Matthew we now possess.”— Craig L. Blomberg, Jesus and the Gospels: An Introduction and Survey, p. 154

Also, Craig Evans affirms that there probably was a Hebrew Matthew:

“What the Elder says of Matthew, who wrote an ordered account in Hebrew, can hardly be in reference to our Greek Matthew. Extant canonical Matthew was composed in Greek; it is not a translation of a Semitic original. But this does not mean that the tradition of a Hebrew Matthew is a myth. On the contrary, the tradition of a Hebrew Matthew is early, widespread, and well attested.”— Craig A. Evans, Jesus and the Manuscripts, p. 21

Also, Craig Evans and Craig Blomberg point to a 14th-century manuscript of Shem Tov ben Isaac (Evans, Jesus and the Manuscripts, 115–126; Blomberg, Jesus and the Gospels, 155).

Shem Tov was writing in Hebrew, and he preserved the text of Matthew’s Gospel in Hebrew. And it seemed to represent certain independent renderings of an older Hebrew tradition.

Also, as of the time of Saint Jerome, a copy of the Hebrew Matthew was still present.

“Matthew, who is also Levi, from a publican to an apostle, was the first to compose a gospel of Christ in Judea for the sake of those who had believed from the circumcision in Hebrew itself, is preserved up to the present day at the library of Caesarea which Pamphilus the martyr very diligently collected. I had leave also given me to copy it by the Nazaraeans in Beroea a city of Syria who use this work. In which it is to be observed that whenever the evangelist… uses testimonies of the Old Testament, he does not follow the authority of the Septuagint, but of the Hebrew.”— Jerome, On Illustrious Men 3

And if indeed there was not a Hebrew Matthew and it was different from the Greek Matthew, it wouldn’t make sense for multiple people to say that Matthew was written in Hebrew; they would have just said that it was written in Greek, if in fact they were trying to hide something.

Why Would Matthew Use Mark?

Lastly, another objection might be that, since Matthew was a disciple of Jesus, why would he need Mark’s writing to write his, when Mark was not a disciple of Jesus?

Charles Quarles answers this objection very well:

“If Peter was indeed the authority behind Mark’s gospel, then it is not surprising that Matthew would utilize Mark. Peter was a member of the inner circle of disciples, often served as their leader, and emerged as the most prominent member of the 12 in the history of the early church.”— Charles L. Quarles, Matthew, Evangelical Biblical Theology Commentary (EBTC), p. 6

The Gospel According to Mark

Now let’s take a look at the Gospel according to Mark.

It’s important to know that Mark was not a disciple of Jesus; however, he was mentored by Peter, and he was Peter’s interpreter:

“She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you; and so does Mark my son.” — 1 Peter 5:13 (NKJV)

And later on, Mark became Paul’s companion:

“And Barnabas and Saul returned from Jerusalem when they had fulfilled their ministry, and they also took with them John whose surname was Mark.” — Acts 12:25 (NKJV)
“Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you, with Mark the cousin of Barnabas (about whom you received instructions: if he comes to you, welcome him),” — Colossians 4:10 (NKJV)

So therefore, since the Gospel according to Mark goes back to Jesus’s disciple Peter, then Mark is in fact based on an eyewitness account.

About the anonymity of the Gospel according to Mark, Craig Blomberg says this:

“It is hard to believe that the oldest traditions would uniformly associate the first two Gospels with Mark and Luke without some good historical reason, because neither was otherwise viewed as a significant figure in first-century Christianity.”— Craig L. Blomberg, The Historical Reliability of the New Testament, p. 9

Early Witnesses to Mark

Just as for Matthew, there is also external evidence that shows that Mark was indeed the author of the Gospel according to Mark.

“The Elder used to say: Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ. For he neither heard the Lord nor followed him, but afterward, as I said, he followed Peter, who adapted his teaching to the needs of his hearers, but with no intention of giving a connected account of the Lord’s discourses, so that Mark committed no error while he thus wrote some things as he remembered them. For he was careful of one thing, not to omit any of the things which he had heard, and not to state any of them falsely. … So then Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able.”— Papias of Hierapolis (AD 60–139), quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.39.14–16
“The Gospels containing the genealogies were written first, and that the Gospel according to Mark was composed in the following circumstances: Peter having preached the word publicly at Rome, and by the Spirit proclaimed the Gospel, those who were present, who were numerous, entreated Mark… On his composing the Gospel, he handed it to those who had made the request to him;… But John, the last of all, seeing that what was corporeal was set forth in the Gospels, on the entreaty of his intimate friends, and inspired by the Spirit, composed a spiritual Gospel.”— Clement of Alexandria (AD 150–215), quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 6.14.5–7; cf. Clement, Hypotyposeis 6
“After their departure, Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, did also hand down to us in writing what had been preached by Peter.”— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.1
“While that (gospel) which Mark published may be affirmed to be Peter’s whose interpreter Mark was.”— Tertullian, Against Marcion 4.5
“The second Gospel was that according to Mark, who wrote it according to Peter’s instructions.”— Origen, quoted in Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 6.25.5

The Objections to Mark’s Authorship

That being said, let’s look at arguments against or in favour of Markan authorship.

“One of the points that [Papias] emphatically makes is certainly wrong: he claims that one of Mark’s two primary goals was to tell everything that he had heard from Peter about Jesus. There is simply no way that can be true. The Gospel of Mark takes about two hours to read out loud. After Peter had spent all those months, or years, with Jesus, and after Mark listened to Peter preach about Jesus day and night, are we really to imagine that all Mark heard was two hours’ worth of information?”— Bart D. Ehrman, Jesus, Interrupted, p. 109

Richard Bauckham argues:

“I have also suggested that the subject of both of these verbs of remembering — recalled from memory and related from memory — may be Peter. But this is debatable: many scholars have thought that the person remembering is Mark. Grammatically either reading is possible. … If the subject is not Peter, then this sentence fails actually to convey what Papias clearly intended: that it was Peter’s teaching that Mark wrote down. … This is much better conveyed by saying that Mark wrote what Peter remembered rather than that Mark wrote what he remembered Peter saying.”— Richard Bauckham, Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2nd ed., p. 211

But Richard Carrier goes further to say:

“[Papias] says Mark became Peter’s secretary and faithfully recorded his teachings … but that can’t be true because Peter was a Torah-observant Christian, and Mark’s Gospel advocates against that and in favor of Pauline Christianity (that Peter and Paul were at odds on this point is clear throughout Galatians 2 …).”— Richard C. Carrier, On the Historicity of Jesus, p. 325

Were Peter and Paul at Odds Over the Law?

Now in reality, both Peter and Paul were Torah-observant Christians, because the Torah just speaks of the five Books of Moses. And Jesus said himself that Moses wrote about him:

“For if you believed Moses, you would believe Me; for he wrote about Me.” — John 5:46 (NKJV)

And that he came to fulfil what was written about him in the Law of Moses (that is, the Torah):

“Then He said to them, ‘These are the words which I spoke to you while I was still with you, that all things must be fulfilled which were written in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms concerning Me.’” — Luke 24:44 (NKJV)

And even in the New Testament, there are a lot of times Paul quotes Moses, for example in Romans 10:6-7 (Paul quotes Moses from Deuteronomy 30:12-14):

“But the righteousness of faith speaks in this way, ‘Do not say in your heart, “Who will ascend into heaven?”’ (that is, to bring Christ down from above) or, ‘“Who will descend into the abyss?”’ (that is, to bring Christ up from the dead).” — Romans 10:6-7 (NKJV)

However, I understand what this scholar means when he says Peter was a Torah-observant Christian. He is implying that Peter was a law-observant Christian, but in reality that is not possible, because it is either you believe that you are saved by the law or saved by Christ.

And of course Peter believed he was saved by Christ, as he notes in his epistles:

“knowing that you were not redeemed with corruptible things, like silver or gold, from your aimless conduct received by tradition from your fathers, but with the precious blood of Christ, as of a lamb without blemish and without spot.” — 1 Peter 1:18-19 (NKJV)

However, Peter still followed some Jewish laws (customs), and that is because he is a Jew, and it was okay to follow his Jewish customs as long as they didn’t conflict with the laws of God.

As Jesus clearly states:

“He said to them, ‘All too well you reject the commandment of God, that you may keep your tradition.’” — Mark 7:9 (NKJV)
“He answered and said to them, ‘Why do you also transgress the commandment of God because of your tradition?’” — Matthew 15:3 (NKJV)

And that is why God had to correct Peter in the book of Acts (Acts 10:9-35), because the dietary law and separation from Gentiles looked good, but they conflicted with the laws of God, in that God’s plan was that he would make both Jews and Gentiles one in Christ.

“For He Himself is our peace and our bond of unity. He who made both groups—[Jews and Gentiles]—into one body and broke down the barrier, the dividing wall [of spiritual antagonism between us], by abolishing in His [own crucified] flesh the hostility caused by the Law with its commandments contained in ordinances [which He satisfied]; so that in Himself He might make the two into one new man, thereby establishing peace. And [that He] might reconcile them both [Jew and Gentile, united] in one body to God through the cross, thereby putting to death the hostility.” — Ephesians 2:14-16 (AMP)

And that is why Paul rebuked Peter, because Peter already knew this, but at that point he acted hypocritically. So in the context of Galatians 2, Paul wasn’t saying that Peter was a law-observing Christian or that Peter was made righteous by the law; he was rebuking Peter for being hypocritical, in that Peter wouldn’t normally do that, but for fear of people (other Jews) he acted in that way:

“Now when Peter had come to Antioch, I withstood him to his face, because he was to be blamed; for before certain men came from James, he would eat with the Gentiles; but when they came, he withdrew and separated himself, fearing those who were of the circumcision. And the rest of the Jews also played the hypocrite with him, so that even Barnabas was carried away with their hypocrisy. But when I saw that they were not straightforward about the truth of the gospel, I said to Peter before them all, ‘If you, being a Jew, live in the manner of Gentiles and not as the Jews, why do you compel Gentiles to live as Jews? We who are Jews by nature, and not sinners of the Gentiles, knowing that a man is not justified by the works of the law but by faith in Jesus Christ, even we have believed in Christ Jesus, that we might be justified by faith in Christ and not by the works of the law; for by the works of the law no flesh shall be justified. But if, while we seek to be justified by Christ, we ourselves also are found sinners, is Christ therefore a minister of sin? Certainly not! For if I build again those things which I destroyed, I make myself a transgressor. For I through the law died to the law that I might live to God. I have been crucified with Christ; it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me; and the life which I now live in the flesh I live by faith in the Son of God, who loved me and gave Himself for me. I do not set aside the grace of God; for if righteousness comes through the law, then Christ died in vain.’” — Galatians 2:11-21 (NKJV)

The Gospel According to Luke

Over to the Gospel according to Luke.

Luke was a Gentile physician:

“Luke the beloved physician and Demas greet you.” — Colossians 4:14 (NKJV)

And he was one of the travel companions of Paul, but he was not a disciple of Jesus.

The argument that the Gospel according to Luke was not written by Luke is quite interesting, because, as I said earlier, even if one wanted to give the Gospels names to have authority, I think the name Luke wouldn’t be the first or second name in mind, because, as I said, he wasn’t one of the apostles and he wasn’t a disciple of Jesus.

As some scholars would agree:

“Luke was not, as far as we know, a person of such prominence in the early church as to have two such considerable volumes as these fathered on to him without reason. If people were guessing, would they not be much more likely to come up with an apostle?”— Leon Morris, Luke: An Introduction and Commentary, p. 17
“Whatever may be true of Matthew and Mark, Luke was certainly not published anonymously. No one writes to a named person, Theophilus, and says ‘it seemed good to me to write’ without letting it be known who the writer is.”— Harold Riley, Preface to Luke, p. 109
“…the dedication to Theophilus in Luke’s Gospel means that ‘the name of the author could hardly be omitted from the title’, and Luke’s prologue demands a title with Luke’s name, or else the first person references in Luke 1.1–4 would be left hanging.”— Simon Gathercole, “The Alleged Anonymity of the Canonical Gospels,” The Journal of Theological Studies 69, no. 2 (2018)

Early Witnesses to Luke

Apart from this, external sources all attribute it to Luke:

“As Luke also may be recognised by the style, both to have composed the Acts of the Apostles, and to have translated Paul’s Epistle to the Hebrews.”— Clement of Alexandria, Adumbrationes in Epistolas Canonicas, on 1 Peter 5:13
“Luke also, the companion of Paul, recorded in a book the Gospel preached by him.”— Irenaeus, Against Heresies 3.1
“For even Luke’s form of the Gospel men usually ascribe to Paul. And it may well seem that the works which disciples publish belong to their masters.”— Tertullian, Against Marcion 4.5
“And third, was that according to Luke, the Gospel commended by Paul, which he composed for the converts from the Gentiles.”— Origen, Commentary on Matthew, Book 1
“The third Gospel book, that according to Luke. This physician Luke after Christ’s ascension (resurrection?), since Paul had taken him with him as an expert in the way (of the teaching), composed it in his own name according to (his) thinking. Yet neither did he himself see the Lord in the flesh; and therefore, as he was able to ascertain it, so he begins to tell the story from the birth of John.”— The Muratorian Fragment

The Gospel According to John

The Gospel according to John was written by John the beloved, a disciple of Jesus. The book of John is quite unique in that, unlike Matthew, who was writing to Jews depicting Jesus as the Messiah, Mark, who wrote to those living outside of Israel emphasising Jesus’ supernatural power, and Luke, who wrote to the Gentiles depicting Jesus as the Saviour of all people, John’s aim of writing was to depict Jesus as the Son of God.

“And truly Jesus did many other signs in the presence of His disciples, which are not written in this book; but these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in His name.” — John 20:30-31 (NKJV)

Early Witnesses to John

Just like the other Gospels, there is external evidence that shows that John was indeed the author of his work:

“For none of these plainly declared His Godhead, as John does when he makes Him say, I am the light of the world, I am the way and the truth and the life, I am the resurrection, I am the door, I am the good shepherd.”— Origen, Commentary on the Gospel of John 1.6
“The same authority of the apostolic churches will afford evidence to the other Gospels also, which we possess equally through their means, and according to their usage — I mean the Gospels of John and Matthew…”— Tertullian, Against Marcion 4.5
“The fourth Gospel is that of John, one of the disciples. When his fellow disciples and bishops entreated him, he said, ‘Fast ye now with me for the space of three days, and let us recount to each other whatever may be revealed to each of us.’ On the same night, it was revealed to Andrew, one of the apostles, that John should narrate all things in his own name as they called them to mind.”— The Muratorian Fragment

What About John 21:24?

However, Bart Ehrman would argue that John couldn’t have written the Gospel of John because of John 21:24:

“This is the disciple who testifies of these things, and wrote these things; and we know that his testimony is true.” — John 21:24 (NKJV)
“For some reason I have never been able to understand, readers frequently take this verse to mean that the beloved disciple is claiming to have written the Gospel of John. But that’s not the case at all. The author cannot be the disciple himself because he clearly differentiates between himself (‘we’) and the beloved disciple (‘his’ testimony).”— Bart D. Ehrman, Jesus Before the Gospels, p. 108

Richard Bauckham argues against this:

“John 21:24 employs a particular Johannine idiom that occurs elsewhere in the Johannine writings (i.e., the Gospel and the three letters of John) in John 3:11; 1 John 1:1–5; 4:14; and 3 John 9–10, 12.” “[John uses] the first person plural (pronouns and verbs) as a substitute for ‘I’.”— Richard Bauckham, Jesus and the Eyewitnesses, 2nd ed., pp. 370–371

Another scholar argues against his point, but does not support the notion that, because it wasn’t John the beloved that wrote John 21:24, it wasn’t him that wrote the Gospel according to John.

“It is difficult to see how much more explicit v. 24 could be that the disciple whom Jesus loved wrote the Gospel. … [Bauckham’s] argument concerning what he dubs the ‘we of authoritative testimony’ is fascinating and worth considering … If I am not entirely convinced by Bauckham’s argument about 21:24, it is only because he does not present any specific example in which this ‘we of authoritative testimony’ is immediately followed by a switch to the third person. This is not to say that the third person by itself is problematic or unlikely to be written by the author, only that the combination of ‘we’ and ‘he’ in the same sentence seems to me probably best interpreted as being written by someone other than the author himself. Hence there may be a short coda to the Gospel of two or at most three verses written by another hand. But even if we grant that 21:24 is written by someone other than the author, the verse itself says that the disciple Jesus loved wrote what comes before.”— Lydia McGrew, The Eye of the Beholder, pp. 129–130

And to a good degree, I support Lydia McGrew. Let’s take for example the book of Deuteronomy (Deuteronomy 34:1-8), attributed to be written by Moses: the last verse said that Moses died. Of course Moses didn’t write that last verse; it was probably Joshua, but the rest of it is attributed to Moses.

No Anonymous Copies, No Ancient Debate

So in conclusion, what we see is that the four Gospels are each attributed to Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. And there is no time that these four Gospels were attributed to another person, just as Brant Pitre would agree:

“That’s what you get with a truly anonymous book of the New Testament: actual anonymous manuscripts, and actual ancient debates over who wrote it. But that’s precisely what you don’t find when it comes to the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John. No anonymous copies, and … no debate among ancient Christians over who wrote the Gospels. … In short, the theory of the anonymous Gospels suffers from not only a lack of manuscript evidence but also from a lack of logic.”— Brant Pitre, The Case for Jesus, p. 22

Did Gaius of Rome Credit John’s Gospel to Cerinthus?

Lastly, there is a claim that Gaius of Rome attributed the fourth Gospel to Cerinthus, and we can’t ascertain that claim. And why is that?

Because Eusebius quotes Gaius in his work:

“We have understood that at this time Cerinthus, the author of another heresy, made his appearance. Gaius, whose words we quoted above, in the Disputation which is ascribed to him, writes as follows concerning this man: But Cerinthus also, by means of revelations which he pretends were written by a great apostle, brings before us marvelous things which he falsely claims were shown him by angels; and he says that after the resurrection the kingdom of Christ will be set up on earth, and that the flesh dwelling in Jerusalem will again be subject to desires and pleasures.”— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.28.1–3

And from the above we see that yes, Gaius thought that the Apocalypse of John (the book of Revelation) was not written by John the beloved but rather by Cerinthus, as Eusebius goes on to say:

“And Dionysius, who was bishop of the parish of Alexandria in our day, in the second book of his work on the Promises, where he says some things concerning the Apocalypse of John which he draws from tradition, mentions this same man in the following words: But (they say that) Cerinthus, who founded the sect which was called, after him, the Cerinthian, desiring reputable authority for his fiction, prefixed the name. For the doctrine which he taught was this: that the kingdom of Christ will be an earthly one.”— Eusebius, Ecclesiastical History 3.28.5–6

However, it’s not written that Gaius believed that the Gospel according to John was not written by John.

So then where does this claim come from?

We find out that the statement comes through a much later Syriac source, Dionysius bar Salibi (12th century), who preserves material attributed to Hippolytus.

The quotation is essentially:

“A man named Gaius appeared, who said that the Gospel was not John’s, nor the Apocalypse, but that they were the work of the heretic Cerinthus.”

However, see Charles E. Hill, The Johannine Corpus in the Early Church (Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2004), chap. 4, “Gaius of Rome and the Johannine Controversy,” pp. 172–204. The chapter abstract states:

“Gaius of Rome, thought to have been a presbyter or bishop in the early years of the third century, is said to have rejected both the Johannine Gospel and Apocalypse, attributing one or both to the heretic Cerinthus. Gaius very probably did reject the Apocalypse, but when legend and later scholarly accretions are untangled, the opinion that he opposed the Fourth Gospel is seen to be based upon very shaky evidence.”— Charles E. Hill, The Johannine Corpus in the Early Church, ch. 4

So we can conclude by saying each Gospel was actually attributed to its original author.

Frequently Asked Questions

Were the Gospels originally anonymous?

The Gospel writers do not name themselves in the text, but that was normal for ancient authors such as Plutarch, Philo and Tacitus. No surviving Gospel manuscript carries a different author's name, and the only one without a title (P1) has simply lost its heading.

Why does it matter that Matthew, Mark and Luke were not famous names?

If second-century Christians had invented authors to give the Gospels authority, they would have chosen leading figures such as Peter, Paul or James. Matthew was a tax collector, and Mark and Luke were not apostles at all, so their names only make sense if they were the real authors.

Was the Gospel of Matthew written in Hebrew or Greek?

Papias, Irenaeus, Origen and Jerome all say Matthew first wrote in Hebrew. Scholars such as Craig Blomberg and Craig Evans suggest Matthew wrote an earlier Hebrew work and later produced the Greek Gospel we have, drawing on Mark, who recorded Peter's preaching.

Does John 21:24 prove John did not write the Gospel of John?

No. Richard Bauckham argues that John uses "we" in place of "I" elsewhere in his writings. And even if another hand added that verse, as Lydia McGrew notes, the verse itself says the beloved disciple wrote what comes before it, much as Deuteronomy 34 records Moses' death at the end of a book attributed to him.
