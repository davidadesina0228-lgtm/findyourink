The Valley of Baca and Bakkah

Infact some go on to say that the Baca in the psalms correlates with Bakkah/Mecca proving that it existed but let’s see if this claim is true.

Psalm 84:6

Transliteration (Latin Script):

'Ovrei be'emeg habakha' mayan yeshituhu, gam berkhöt ya'teh möreh.

King James Version (KJV) Translation:

“Who passing through the valley of Baca make it a well; the rain also filleth the pools.”

In its syntax, the subject is the pilgrims-“those passing through the Valley of Baka.” The verb 'ovrei (“those who pass through”) conveys motion toward the sanctuary, not settlement within it. The noun bakhã' derives from the Hebrew root noa, meaning to weep or tears; thus “the Valley of Baka” literally means “the Valley of Weeping.” It depicts a landscape of hardship that must be traversed on the way to encounter God. The next phrase, ma'yān yeshituhu (“they make it a spring”), introduces transformation-the place of sorrow becomes a source of water, a symbol of renewal. The line gam beräkhöt ya'teh möreh adds a theological layer: “even the early rain covers it with blessings.” The yöreh, the autumn rain, marks the beginning of the fertile season in the Promised Land imagery of divine grace and life.

Ancient versions reinforce this symbolic reading. The Greek Septuagint renders the phrase as thv колаба той клавийо, -“the valley of weeping”— and the Latin Vulgate as valle lacrimarum, “the valley of tears.” The King James Version follows a slightly different Hebrew vocalization of n1272, taking it as berēkhöt (“pools”) rather than beräkhöt (“blessings”), hence: “Who passing through the valley of Baca make it a well; the rain also filleth the pools.” This choice adds vivid visual imagery-pools filled with rain-but the core meaning remains the same: sorrow turned to refreshment. Stylistically, the psalm moves from yearning (vv. 2-4), to the blessedness of the pilgrims (vv. 5-7), and finally to prayer and affirmation (vv. 8-12). The “Valley of Baka” stands at the heart of that structure as a rhetorical pivot, expressing transition. It is not a geographical coordinate but a spiritual metaphor within Israel’s worship poetry. In Israel’s religious landscape, water, early rain, and springs consistently symbolize divine mercy and restoration. Thus, when the pilgrims “make it a spring,” the text portrays faith transforming suffering into joy.

Arabic Text:

إنّ أَوْلَ بَيْتِ وَضِعَ لِلنَاسِ للّذِي بِبَكَةَ مُبَارَكًا وَهُدى لَلْعَالَمِينَ

Transliteration:

Inna awwala baytin wudia li-n-nāsi lalladi bi-Bakkah, mubãrakan wa hudan lil alamin.

English (Sahih International):

“Indeed, the first House [of worship] established for mankind was that at Bakkah - blessed and a guidance for the worlds.”

From a linguistic and historical perspective, this verse is striking. The Qur’an here uses Bakkah (بكة) instead of Makkah (مكة), although later Islamic tradition treats both as identical. Yet, philologically, the two terms have distinct roots and meanings. The Arabic triliteral root (bakka) means to press down, to crowd, or to crush. Classical lexicons such as Lisān al-'Arab and Tãj al-'Arus confirm that the verb bakka was used to describe physical pressure or congestion, often associated with a crowded gathering. There is no semantic or morphological link between bakkah and the Hebrew baka (n22), which means to weep. The resemblance in sound between Bakkah and Baka is therefore a phonetic coincidence, not a linguistic connection. Historically, no inscription, papyrus, or pre-Islamic poem mentions a place called Bakkah. The rich corpus of Arabian inscriptions from the Nabataean, Sabaean, Minaean, and Qatabanian cultures- all flourishing centuries before Islam -contains hundreds of toponyms across the Arabian Peninsula, yet none reference Bakkah.

This absence is notable given that those inscriptions recorded even small trade stops and sanctuaries. The silence of the epigraphic record suggests that Bakkah was not a known pre-Islamic designation but rather a term that appears first within the Qur’anic revelation itself.

Classical Muslim exegetes such as al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, and al-Qurtubi recognized this issue. To harmonize the text, they asserted that Bakkah was simply another name for Makkah-some said it referred to the specific area of the Ka'bah, while Makkah referred to the surrounding valley. However, this explanation was based on internal interpretation, not external evidence. None of these scholars could cite historical documents, ancient maps, or linguistic parallels that predate Islam to substantiate the identification. Their reasoning rested solely on the phonetic similarity between the two words.

The Silence of Ancient Geography

The geographical settings of these two texts belong to entirely different worlds. The “Valley of Baka” in the Psalms is rooted in the landscape of Canaan-the southern region of ancient Israel, within a realistic distance for pilgrims traveling to Jerusalem. Its scenery evokes limestone hills, shallow valleys, and pilgrimage routes that naturally converge toward Zion. This is the world of Judah and Benjamin, not the world of long desert caravans.

Mecca, by contrast, lies in the Hijaz-the granitic mountain belt running parallel to the Red Sea, about 1,200 kilometers south of Jerusalem in a straight line, and much farther by ancient land routes. The Meccan valley is enclosed by rugged hills, set inland from the coast, with only limited seasonal water sources. Around the time of Abraham, roughly 2000 B.C., archaeological reconstructions show that western Arabia had very sparse settlement: no walled cities, no trade archives, no monumental sanctuaries comparable to the urban centers of the Levant. Egyptian, Assyrian, Babylonian, and Canaanite chronicles-all meticulous in listing border towns and trading posts-never mention any place in the heart of the Hijaz that could be identified as Mecca or Bakkah.

The classical geographers-Strabo (1st century B.C.), Pliny the Elder (1st century A.D.), and Claudius Ptolemy (2nd century A.D.) -are our primary sources for the topography of pre-Islamic Arabia. Each of them produced monumental works that attempted to map the known world in considerable detail.

  • Strabo, in his Geographica, described Arabia extensively, especially its northern and southern regions, naming cities such as Nabataea, Taima, Yathrib, Gerrha, and Minae.
  • Pliny the Elder, in Naturalis Historia, outlined the trade routes that crossed the peninsula from the Persian Gulf to the Red Sea, listing commercial hubs such as Carmania, Gerrha, Mariaba, and Nekhla-yet not once mentioning Mecca.
  • Ptolemy, in his Geögraphike Hyphegesis (Geographia), went even further, providing latitude-and-longitude coordinates for hundreds of towns, rivers, and mountains throughout Arabia. He recorded over fifty toponyms in the region now known as the Hijaz, including Yathrippa (Yathrib/Medina), Macoraba (which some later Muslim writers tried to identify with Mecca, though that interpretation is widely disputed), Lathrippa, Thelme, and Thaima.

Yet none of these authors ever mention a city explicitly named “Mecca” or “Bakkah.” In historiographical terms, the absence of such a name in the three most comprehensive geographical sources of the ancient world constitutes evidence of absence-not merely missing data, but a meaningful silence within corpora that were otherwise meticulous in recording major trade centers and oasis towns.

This absence carries particular weight because, if Mecca had truly been an international trading hub or ancient sanctuary from the time of Abraham, as Islamic tradition claims, it would almost certainly appear in these Greco-Roman works that focus heavily on Arabian commerce. Yet even the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century A.D.)—a maritime manual detailing Red Sea ports and inland markets-lists Leuke Kome and Mochorba along the northwestern coast but makes no mention of Mecca at all.

Between the first and sixth centuries A.D., Arabia produced an extraordinarily rich epigraphic record. Archaeologists and epigraphists have discovered tens of thousands of inscriptions across the peninsula, written in a variety of local scripts-Nabataean in the northwest, Thamudic and Safaitic in the central and northern deserts, and Sabaean, Minaean, and Qatabanian in the south (modern Yemen).

These texts collectively represent the administrative, economic, and religious activity of pre-Islamic Arabia. They commemorate kings and queens, record trade transactions, dedications to deities, military campaigns, and even casual graffiti left by travelers and merchants. Within this immense body of evidence, numerous Arabian cities are well-attested:

  • Dedan (modern al-Ula), capital of the Lihyanite kingdom;
  • Taima, an oasis famous for its Aramaic inscriptions and for being conquered by Nabonidus, king of Babylon in the 6th century B.C.;
  • Najran, a known Christian and trading center;
  • Qaryat al-Faw, an urban settlement in central Arabia with temples, inscriptions, and evidence of regional trade;
  • Marib, Shabwah, and Qana in the south, major nodes of the Sabaean and Hadramitic incense trade networks.

Archaeologically, the Hijaz region- stretching along the Red Sea coast between modern-day Medina and Yemen-shows a very different pattern from that of southern Arabia. Excavations reveal only scattered nomadic camps, small agricultural oases, and limited material culture, but no signs of sustained urban development before the 7th century. The landscape is harsh: mountainous, dry, and without the perennial rivers or fertile valleys that allowed civilizations like Saba or Nabataea to flourish. Whereas southern Arabia boasted irrigation systems such as the Marib Dam and monumental temples dedicated to Almaqah and other deities, the Hijaz remained a cultural backwater, a marginal zone between the more advanced civilizations to its north and south.

The economic geography of Arabia further reinforces this. The famed frankincense and myrrh trade-the economic lifeline of the Arabian Peninsula-was controlled by kingdoms in Yemen and Hadramaut. Ancient sources such as the Periplus of the Erythraean Sea (1st century A.D.) describe the maritime trade routes from ports like Muza, Qana, and Aden that connected southern Arabia with East Africa and India.

The land routes that carried these goods northward to Petra and Gaza were dominated by Nabataean caravans. None of these itineraries, whether coastal or inland, list a stop corresponding to Mecca. Even in later Byzantine and Persian sources describing Arabia’s geography and commerce, Mecca is entirely absent until the Islamic period.

This silence is not a trivial omission; it points to the nonexistence of Mecca as a historically relevant city before the 7th century. If Mecca had been the “center of the world’s trade” or a sacred site founded by Abraham-as later Islamic tradition maintains-it would almost certainly have appeared in the records of civilizations deeply connected to Arabia’s commerce: Egypt, Rome, or the Sabaean kingdoms.

Yet, the documentary and material evidence shows no trace of such a city.

It is only with the emergence of Islam in the early 600s A.D. that Mecca steps onto the historical stage. The earliest datable references to the city appear within the Qur’an itself and in Islamic tradition, followed by 7th- and 8th-century inscriptions and papyri written by Muslim administrators. Before that, Mecca does not exist in any known source.

The Year of the Elephant

The story of 'Am al-Fil — the “Year of the Elephant” - is one of the most significant narratives in early Islamic historiography because it serves as a chronological anchor and a sacral foundation for the city of Mecca. According to Islamic tradition, this event took place in the year of the Prophet Muhammad’s birth (around 570 CE), when Abrahah al-Ashram, a Christian ruler of Yemen under the Aksumite (Ethiopian) empire, led an army of elephants to destroy the Ka'bah. However, the Qur’an (Surah al-Fil, 105) says that God miraculously destroyed his army by sending flocks of birds (tayran abbil) that pelted them with stones of clay (sijjil).

Abrahah was a real historical figure, known from several Sabaean inscriptions discovered in Marib and Murayghan dating to the mid-6th century CE. These inscriptions confirm his rule over Yemen after Aksum’s invasion under King Kaleb around 525 CE. They describe his repair of the Marib Dam, his military campaigns against rebellious tribes, and his building of a grand cathedral called al-Qullays in Sana'a

However, none of these inscriptions mention an expedition to the Hijaz, the region where Mecca is located. Archaeological and epigraphic evidence limits Abrahah’s influence to southern and southwestern Arabia - particularly Najran and the highlands of Yemen - far from Mecca, which lies over 1,000 kilometers to the north across one of the most inhospitable deserts in the world

Epigraphists such as Jacques Ryckmans, Christian Robin, and G.W. Bowersock have thoroughly examined over thirty inscriptions relating to Abrahah’s reign. They unanimously conclude that all his documented campaigns were internal to Yemen and its neighboring regions, not toward Mecca. No inscription, no chronicle, and no foreign source mentions a march of elephants to the Hijaz.

There is no corroborating Byzantine, Persian, or Himyarite source from the 6th century that records a military campaign involving elephants against Mecca. This is especially striking given that Byzantine and Aksumite sources were otherwise meticulous in chronicling regional military affairs in Arabia and the Red Sea. If such an extraordinary event had occurred — a Christian army marching with elephants across Arabia - it would have been politically and theologically noteworthy to neighboring powers. Its complete absence from all external records strongly suggests that the event never took place.

Furthermore, if Mecca had already been an important religious or commercial hub at that time, one would expect it to appear in trade documents, itineraries, or correspondence between Arabia and the wider Mediterranean world. Yet Mecca is entirely missing from all pre-Islamic records.

From a logistical standpoint, the story is physically implausible. The journey from Sana'a to Mecca crosses mountainous terrain and arid plains devoid of permanent water sources. Modern geomorphological studies show that even the major oases such as Najran and Bisha had only limited seasonal water at that time.

An African elephant requires between 100-200 liters of water and 70-150 kilograms of food per day. No known infrastructure, irrigation system, or caravan supply route in 6th-century Arabia could sustain even a small contingent of elephants over a 1,000-kilometer march. Moreover, Aksumite military practice never included the long-distance use of elephants in desert warfare. Elephants appear in Indian, Persian, and sub-Saharan armies, but not in the arid Arabian context. The ecology of the Hijaz simply could not support such an expedition.

Modern historians interpret 'Am al-Fil as a theological or etiological legend rather than a historical event.

  • Patricia Crone, in Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam (1987), argues that the story was created to fill the historical silence surrounding Mecca’s pre-Islamic past - a symbolic act of divine protection marking Muhammad’s birth.
  • G.W. Bowersock, in The Throne of Adulis (2013), confirms Abrahah’s historical existence but notes that the “Year of the Elephant” legend likely emerged later as a pious myth reflecting God’s defense of His sanctuary rather than an actual military event.
  • Christian Robin interprets the narrative as a theological reworking of a local conflict, transforming an otherwise ordinary episode into a cosmic sign of divine intervention.

Such reinterpretation follows a broader ancient pattern of sacralizing geography - projecting miraculous events backward in time to legitimize a place’s holiness. Similar patterns appear in Jewish and Christian literature, where the birth of a prophet is surrounded by signs in the heavens or divine deliverance from enemies.

Across all disciplines - epigraphy, archaeology, military ecology, and textual analysis — the “Year of the Elephant” collapses as history. It survives instead as a foundational legend, expressing theological meaning rather than empirical memory.

Within early Islam, the story functioned to sanctify Mecca’s role by associating the Prophet’s birth with an unmistakable act of divine protection. In academic terms, the problem with the 'Ăm al-Fil narrative is not hostility toward Islam but the absence of independent evidence. The principle of critical historiography demands corroboration from contemporary, non-Islamic sources-and none exist. Consequently, historians view the episode as part of Islam’s sacred historiography, not verifiable secular history. Mecca’s portrayal as an ancient Abrahamic center and Abrahah’s supposed attack both emerge from within Islamic theological memory, not from the demonstrable record of the ancient world.
