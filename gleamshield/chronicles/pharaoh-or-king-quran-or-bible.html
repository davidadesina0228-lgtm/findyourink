Pharaoh or King: Who Got It Right, the Quran or the Bible?

By Angel Kanu — July 27, 2026

Key Takeaways

  • The Bible has two Pharaoh encounters: Joseph’s in Genesis (Middle Kingdom era) and Moses’s in Exodus (New Kingdom era).
  • “Pharaoh” comes from the Egyptian pr-aa (“Great House”); it only became a title for the king from the 18th Dynasty onward (c. 1400s BCE) — Moses’s era, not Joseph’s.
  • Genesis uses “Pharaoh” retroactively because Moses wrote in the language his own generation understood — the same reasoning behind saying “Julius Caesar ruled France.”
  • Quranic Arabic grammar treats “Fir’awn” as a proper name rather than a title — meaning the Quran presents it as if it were the ruler’s actual name, which historically it was not.
  • The claim that Muhammad “corrected” the Bible with special knowledge does not hold up under either Egyptology or the Quran’s own grammar.

The Claim: Did the Quran Correct the Bible?

I once talked to a Muslim guy and he said the Quran came to correct the Bible, that the Bible referred to the king in Genesis as pharaoh when there was no Pharaoh then. And the Quran came and corrected it, that how would Mohammed have known this if the Quran is not the word of Allah.

So now we would look at the claim carefully.

Two Pharaoh Encounters in the Bible

So now in the Bible there are two encounters with pharaoh - one in Genesis and the other in exodus.

The one in Genesis was with Joseph -

And Pharaoh said to Joseph, ‘I have had a dream, and there is no one who can interpret it. But I have heard it said of you that you can understand a dream, to interpret it.’ So Joseph answered Pharaoh, saying, ‘It is not in me; God will give Pharaoh an answer of peace.’” — Genesis 41:14-16 (NKJV)

And the one in exodus was with Moses -

“Afterward Moses and Aaron went in and told Pharaoh, ‘Thus says the Lord God of Israel: “Let My people go, that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.”’” — Exodus 5:1 (NKJV)

The Etymology of “Pharaoh”

So the word "Pharaoh" comes from the ancient Egyptian term pr-aʹ, which literally translates to "Great House" or "palace"

In the original Hebrew, it was transliterated as Par'ōh, which evolved into the English word Pharaoh.

1. Joseph’s Era (The Middle Kingdom / Hyksos Period)

The Timeline: Historians and biblical scholars generally place the story of Joseph centuries before the Exodus, during Egypt's Middle Kingdom or the Hyksos period (roughly 2000–1600 B.C.E.).

The Reality: During this time, the word pr-aʹ (the root of Pharaoh) strictly meant "Great House" and referred only to the physical palace buildings.

The Title: The actual title for the ruler during Joseph's lifetime was Nesu or Nesu-bit (meaning King). No one living in Egypt during Joseph's life would have addressed the king as "Pharaoh".

2. Moses’s Era (The New Kingdom / Exodus Period)

The Timeline: The Book of Exodus takes place much later, during Egypt's New Kingdom period (roughly 1500–1200 B.C.E.), often tied by historians to rulers like Thutmose III or Ramesses II.

The Reality: It was precisely during this New Kingdom era (starting around the 18th Dynasty, c. 1539 B.C.E.) that Egyptians began using "Great House" metonymically to refer to the person of the king.

The Title: Therefore, during the events of the Exodus, the term was actively used in Egypt as a respectful title for the monarch.

Why Does Genesis Use “Pharaoh”?

So now Genesis and Exodus are both attributed to moses, and moses knew the story of joseph through a vision of God because he was not alive during that period. So, imagine moses is writing this vision of joseph during his time, he would use the same phrase/word that is understood in his time.

And let's imagine, moses wrote joseph's story and used the word king. We know that Hebrew Bible was compiled long after the events of Joseph took place. By the time the Israelite scribes were writing down the Genesis narratives, "Pharaoh" (par'ōh) had become the standard, universal Hebrew word for the ruler of Egypt. Rather than using obsolete Middle Kingdom terminology that their readers wouldn't recognize, the biblical authors used the common language of their own day. It is the modern equivalent of a historian writing that "Julius Caesar ruled France"—even though the region was called Gaul at the time, using "France" makes it immediately understandable to a modern audience

What Arabic Grammar Actually Shows

Now let's check these gradually, according to Arabic grammar, there is a rule of thumb.

Rule of Thumb

General/Standalone Title: Use "al-" (e.g., al-Wazīr arrived).

Title of a Specific Entity: Remove "al-" and attach the title directly to the entity it governs (e.g., Wazīr al-Difā' / The Minister of Defense)

the word فِرْعَوْنُ (Fir'awn / Pharaoh) in this verse does not follow either rule of thumb because in Arabic grammar, it behaves like a personal proper name, not an official title

1. It Treats Fir'awn as a Personal Name

If Fir'awn were functioning as a standard title (like "the king" or "the minister"), it would need to follow the first rule of thumb and take the definite article—becoming Al-Fir'awn (الفرعون)

However, throughout the entire Qur'an, the word never takes "al-". It is always treated grammatically as a proper noun (the specific name of the individual Moses is addressing)

2. The Clue in the Vocative Particles

The verse reads: يَٰفِرْعَوْنُ (Yā-Fir'awn / "O Pharaoh").

In Arabic grammar, when you call someone directly using يَا (Yā / "O..."), you attach it directly to a personal proper name (e.g., Yā Muhammadu, Yā Mūsā).

If you want to call someone by an official title or role, the grammar changes. You cannot say Yā Al-Malik or Yā Al-Ra'īs. You have to add an extra pronoun particle, resulting in phrases like Yā ayyuhā al-malik (O you the king).

Because the text says Yā-Fir'awn directly, the Arabic language structures it identically to addressing a person by their first name

3. Grammatical Classification (Mamnu' min al-Sarf)

Linguistically, Fir'awn is classified in Arabic grammar as a diptote (Mamnu' min al-Sarf). This is a specific grammatical category reserved for non-Arabic proper names (like Ibrāhīm, Yūsuf, or Ismā'īl). Titles do not fall into this category—only foreign proper names do.

So this means that according to the Quran when Mohammed said, Moses called the ruler of Egypt Pharaoh, it was not because he thought it was his title, he actually called him pharaoh because he thought the ruler of Egypt's name was Pharaoh, which is wrong.

So, in summary, the Quran didn't come to correct anything and using this as a claim to show that the Quran is the word of Allah because Mohammed didn't know, doesn't work. Because during Mohammed's time, oral history was still rampant, so when Mohammed would have heard the story, he probably thought the ruler of Egypt's name was pharaoh.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did the Quran correct a mistake in the Bible about “Pharaoh”?

No. The Bible uses "Pharaoh" in Genesis because Moses, writing centuries after Joseph, used the term standard in his own day — the same way a modern writer might say "Julius Caesar ruled France," using the familiar name rather than the era-accurate one. By the time Genesis was written down, "Pharaoh" was already the universal Hebrew word for Egypt's ruler.

When did “Pharaoh” actually become a title for the king of Egypt?

Egyptologists date the shift to the New Kingdom, starting around the 18th Dynasty (c. 1400s BCE). Before that, in the Middle Kingdom period when Joseph would have lived, the actual royal title was "Nesu" or "Nesu-bit" — "Pharaoh" (pr-aa) meant only "Great House," referring to the palace itself, not the person of the king.

Does the Quran’s use of “Pharaoh” prove Muhammad had special knowledge the Bible lacked?

No — the opposite. Arabic grammar shows the Quran treats "Fir'awn" as a diptote proper name, like Ibrahim or Yusuf, never taking the definite article "al-" and addressed directly as "O Pharaoh" rather than "O you the king." This indicates the Quran presents Pharaoh as if it were the ruler's personal name, which is historically inaccurate — not a correction of the Bible.
